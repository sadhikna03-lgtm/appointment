Reading the room: teaching conversational turn-taking

Social 4 min read

Turn-taking looks simple but relies on a stack of skills — timing, cues, and interest in what the other person just said. Here's how it's built.

A smooth conversation looks effortless, but it's actually a layered skill: knowing when to speak, when to listen, how to read that someone else wants a turn, and how to respond to what was actually said rather than just waiting for a gap to say something unrelated. For some children and teens, especially those with social communication difficulties, each of these needs to be taught directly.

The skills underneath "taking turns"

  • Timing: recognizing the natural pause that signals it's someone else's turn to speak.
  • Topic maintenance: responding to what the other person said, rather than switching to an unrelated favorite topic.
  • Nonverbal cues: reading body language, tone, and facial expression that signal interest, confusion, or a desire to speak.
  • Repair: noticing when a conversation has stalled or gone off track, and knowing how to bring it back.

Starting small: structured practice first

Turn-taking is often taught first in a very structured format — a simple back-and-forth game, or a conversation with a visual cue for whose turn it is — before being applied to open, unstructured conversation. This gives the pattern time to become familiar before it needs to work under the pressure of a real social moment.

Video modeling and self-review

For older children and teens, watching short video clips of conversations — including their own, with permission — can make abstract cues concrete. Seeing what "waiting too long" or "interrupting" actually looks like is often more effective than being told about it.

Practicing where it counts

The real goal is generalizing the skill to unscripted settings — the lunch table, a group project, a phone call with a friend. Therapy usually moves toward these settings gradually, once the underlying pieces are solid enough to hold up without heavy support.

Why this pairs well with other therapy goals

Social communication support often runs alongside work on articulation, language, or fluency, since confidence in conversation affects how much a child is willing to practice everything else.
