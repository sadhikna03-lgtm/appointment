Recovery

Supporting a loved one through aphasia recovery

Recovery 5 min read

Aphasia changes how someone communicates, not what they understand or feel. A few adjustments at home make daily conversation far less frustrating for everyone.

One of the hardest things for families to internalize after a stroke or brain injury is that aphasia affects the mechanics of language — finding words, forming sentences, sometimes understanding speech — without touching a person's intelligence, personality, or emotions. Conversations can look very different, but the person having them hasn't changed.

Slow down, but don't simplify the content

Speaking more slowly gives someone with aphasia time to process — but there's no need to talk to an adult like a child. Simpler sentence structure helps; simpler ideas usually aren't necessary and can feel patronizing.

Let silence do some of the work

The instinct to fill a pause by guessing the word someone is searching for is understandable, but it often short-circuits their own process. A few extra seconds of quiet, with a patient expression rather than a rescue, usually gets better results — and always ask first whether they'd like help finding a word.

Use more than words

  • Gestures, pointing, and facial expression all carry real communicative weight — encourage rather than discourage them.
  • Written key words, a pen and paper, or drawings can support a conversation that's otherwise stalling on a single word.
  • Photos and familiar objects can anchor a conversation when abstract words are hard to retrieve.

Ask yes/no questions when things stall

Open-ended questions ("What do you want for dinner?") ask a lot of a person rebuilding word-finding. Offering two clear choices, or a yes/no question, often keeps a conversation moving without adding pressure.

Protect the relationship, not just the exercises

Formal practice matters, but so does ordinary conversation that isn't therapy — a shared joke, a comment on the weather, a story about the day. Recovery tends to go better when communication still feels like connection, not just a set of drills to get through.

Coordinated care matters

Speech-language therapy after a stroke works best alongside the rest of a person's medical team — neurologists, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists all play a part, and progress reviews help keep everyone aligned on what's working.
