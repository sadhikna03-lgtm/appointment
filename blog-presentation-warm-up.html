Warm-up habits before a big presentation

Voice 3 min read

A few minutes of the right warm-up steadies your voice and your nerves before you speak in front of a room. Here's a routine worth building.

Most people warm up their body before a workout without thinking twice — but skip any warm-up at all before asking their voice to perform under pressure. A short routine before a presentation does two things at once: it prepares the vocal folds physically, and it settles nerves through steady, controlled breathing.

Start with breath, not voice

Before making any sound, spend a minute on slow, low breathing — expanding the belly rather than the chest. This is the same breath support that carries a projected voice without strain, and it also activates the body's calming response, which helps with pre-presentation nerves.

A simple five-minute routine

  1. Humming (1 minute): Gentle, relaxed humming up and down in pitch. This eases the vocal folds into vibrating without strain.
  2. Lip trills (1 minute): A "motorboat" sound made by relaxed lips, moving through a comfortable pitch range. This checks that breath support and voice are working together.
  3. Gentle sirens (1 minute): Sliding smoothly from a low pitch to a high pitch and back on an "ng" or "ee" sound, without pushing at the top.
  4. Articulation warm-up (1 minute): A few tongue twisters or simply over-articulated nonsense syllables to loosen the jaw and lips.
  5. A short practice run (1 minute): Say your opening line or two out loud, at the volume and pace you plan to actually use.

What to avoid right before speaking

  • Caffeine right before — it can dry out the vocal folds and add to jitters.
  • Clearing your throat repeatedly, which is actually harder on the vocal folds than a gentle cough or a sip of water.
  • Whispering to "save your voice" — it strains the folds more than speaking normally.

The nerves connection

A shaky voice before speaking publicly is usually less about vocal ability and more about breath — nerves tend to pull breathing up into the chest and make it shallow. The same slow, low breathing used in the warm-up is worth returning to for thirty seconds right before walking up to speak.
